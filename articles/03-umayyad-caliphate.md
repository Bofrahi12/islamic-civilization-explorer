# The Umayyad Caliphate

In 661, Muawiya, the long-serving governor of Syria, became caliph and founded the Umayyad dynasty, the first hereditary ruling house in Islamic history. Moving the capital from Arabia to Damascus, the Umayyads transformed a community of believers into a vast empire — and in doing so, created the political and cultural template for Islamic civilization.

## From Medina to Damascus

Muawiya was a pragmatist. Rather than ruling from Medina like his predecessors, he governed from Damascus, the cosmopolitan city he had administered for two decades. He built a professional standing army, a navy that challenged Byzantium in the Mediterranean, and a postal and intelligence network to hold the provinces together. Crucially, he nominated his son Yazid as successor, making the caliphate hereditary. This decision secured stability but outraged those who believed leadership should remain with the Prophet's family or be chosen by the community — a grievance that would fuel opposition for the dynasty's entire existence.

## An Empire from Spain to Central Asia

The Umayyads presided over the greatest territorial expansion in Islamic history. In 711, an army led by Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the strait that still bears his name — Jabal Tariq, "the mountain of Tariq," the origin of "Gibraltar" — and within a few years nearly all of Iberia was under Muslim rule. In the east, armies pushed across Central Asia to the borders of China and down into the Indus valley. Only in the west was the advance checked, when Frankish forces under Charles Martel halted a raiding army near Tours in 732. At its height, the caliphate stretched from the Atlantic to the frontiers of China, the largest empire the world had yet seen.

## Administration and Culture

Under Abd al-Malik, who reigned at the turn of the eighth century, the empire acquired a distinctly Islamic character. Arabic replaced Greek and Persian as the language of administration across the provinces, unifying the bureaucracy. He introduced a purely Islamic coinage, replacing coins bearing human images with elegant Arabic inscriptions. His greatest monument is the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed around 691–692: an octagonal shrine of breathtaking beauty, its golden dome still defining the city's skyline and the oldest surviving work of Islamic architecture. His son al-Walid added the Great Mosque of Damascus, another masterpiece of early Islamic art.

## Decline and Fall

Umayyad rule rested on the dominance of Arab Muslims, and non-Arab converts — the mawali — resented paying taxes and serving without the privileges of Arab tribesmen. Shi'a partisans kept alive the claim of the Prophet's family, and in the east, the Abbasids organized a revolutionary movement promising a return to righteous rule. In 750, Abbasid armies defeated the last Umayyad caliph, and the dynasty fell. One prince, Abd al-Rahman, escaped the slaughter, fled across North Africa, and founded an independent Umayyad emirate in Spain — a remarkable second act for the dynasty.

## Key takeaways

- The Umayyads (661–750) created the first Islamic dynasty and moved the capital to Damascus.
- Their conquests built an empire stretching from Spain to Central Asia, the largest of its age.
- Abd al-Malik's reforms — Arabic administration, Islamic coinage, the Dome of the Rock — gave the empire its Islamic identity.
- Overthrown in 750, the dynasty survived in Spain, where a fugitive prince founded a new Umayyad state.
