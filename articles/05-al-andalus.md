# Al-Andalus: Islamic Spain

For nearly eight hundred years, much of the Iberian Peninsula was part of the Islamic world, known in Arabic as al-Andalus. Beginning with the conquest of 711, Muslim Spain became one of medieval Europe's most sophisticated civilizations — a land of great cities, libraries, and palaces where Muslim, Christian, and Jewish cultures interacted in ways that shaped the continent's history.

## The Conquest and the Emirate

In 711, a Muslim army under Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed from North Africa and defeated the Visigothic king at the Battle of Guadalete. Within a few years, almost the entire peninsula had come under Muslim control, governed first as a province of the Umayyad caliphate. When the Abbasids massacred the Umayyad family in 750, one prince — Abd al-Rahman — escaped, crossed North Africa, and landed in Spain, where he founded an independent Umayyad emirate with its capital at Cordoba. His descendants would rule al-Andalus for nearly three centuries, and under them Cordoba grew into a city of mosques, palaces, and bustling markets.

## The Caliphate of Cordoba

In 929, Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed himself caliph, declaring Cordoba the equal of Baghdad. Tenth-century Cordoba was arguably the greatest city in Western Europe: paved and lamplit streets, hundreds of mosques and public baths, and a library said to hold hundreds of thousands of volumes at a time when the largest Christian libraries held mere hundreds. The Great Mosque of Cordoba, with its forest of red-and-white arches, remains one of the masterpieces of Islamic architecture. Scholars at the caliph's court advanced medicine, astronomy, and philosophy, and the city's wealth — drawn from trade, agriculture, and taxation — funded it all.

## Convivencia: Three Cultures

Al-Andalus is famous for the coexistence — convivencia — of Muslims, Christians, and Jews. While the reality was more complex than the romantic image, the interaction was real and fruitful: Jewish scholars like Maimonides wrote philosophy in Arabic, Christian monks translated Arabic science into Latin, and courts patronized poets of all faiths. Toledo, after its capture by Christian forces, became the great translation center through which the works of Aristotle, Ibn Sina, and al-Khwarizmi entered medieval Europe. The intellectual traffic of al-Andalus helped carry classical and Islamic learning into the European mainstream.

## The Nasrid Kingdom and 1492

The caliphate fragmented into small taifa kingdoms in the eleventh century, and the Christian kingdoms of the north gradually pushed south in the long Reconquista. North African dynasties — the Almoravids and Almohads — briefly reunited Muslim Spain, but by the thirteenth century only the Nasrid kingdom of Granada remained. Its rulers built the Alhambra, the breathtaking palace-fortress whose courtyards, fountains, and arabesque carvings represent the final flowering of Andalusi art. In 1492, the Catholic Monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella accepted Granada's surrender, ending Muslim rule in Iberia. The expulsion of Jews and later Muslims closed a remarkable chapter of European history.

## Key takeaways

- Muslim rule in Iberia lasted from 711 to 1492, nearly eight centuries.
- The Caliphate of Cordoba (929–1031) made the city Western Europe's greatest center of learning and wealth.
- Al-Andalus channeled Greek, Arabic, and Islamic science and philosophy into medieval Europe.
- The Alhambra palace in Granada stands as the supreme monument of Andalusi civilization.
