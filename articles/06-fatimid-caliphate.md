# The Fatimid Caliphate

While the Abbasids ruled from Baghdad, a rival caliphate rose in the west. The Fatimids — Ismaili Shi'a Muslims who claimed descent from the Prophet's daughter Fatima — built an empire stretching from North Africa to Syria. Their greatest legacy is Cairo, the city they founded as their capital, which remains one of the great cities of the Islamic world.

## Origins in North Africa

The Fatimid movement began as a secretive Ismaili missionary network operating across the Abbasid Empire. In 909, its leader, Abd Allah al-Mahdi, emerged from hiding in what is now Tunisia and proclaimed himself caliph, directly challenging the Abbasids' claim to lead all Muslims. From their base in Ifriqiya, the Fatimids built a formidable state with a powerful army and navy, raiding Sicily and the Italian coast and extending their influence across North Africa. For half a century they consolidated their Maghrebi empire while looking eastward toward the ultimate prize: Egypt, the richest province of the Abbasid world.

## The Conquest of Egypt and the Founding of Cairo

In 969, the Fatimid general Jawhar led an army into Egypt and took it with little resistance. Just north of the existing settlement of Fustat, he laid out a new royal capital: al-Qahira, "the Victorious" — the city the world knows as Cairo. The Fatimid caliph al-Mu'izz moved his court there in 973, bringing with him the bones of his ancestors and the treasures of his dynasty. Cairo was designed as an imperial city of palaces, mosques, and ceremonial avenues, and it quickly became a rival to Baghdad in splendor. The Fatimids also took the holy cities of Mecca and Medina, making their caliph the most powerful Muslim ruler of his age.

## Al-Azhar and Fatimid Culture

In 970, Jawhar founded a mosque in the new capital that would become one of the most famous institutions in Islamic history: al-Azhar. Established as a center of Ismaili learning, it evolved over the centuries into Sunni Islam's most prestigious university, a role it still holds today — more than a thousand years later. Fatimid Cairo was a city of remarkable religious diversity: the Ismaili rulers employed Sunni, Christian, and Jewish officials, and the city's markets thronged with merchants from across the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean. Fatimid art — rock crystal, luster pottery, carved ivory — was prized from Spain to China.

## Decline and Legacy

Fatimid power waned as later caliphs proved weaker and as the Seljuk Turks and then the Crusaders pressed in from the east. In 1171, the Kurdish general Salah al-Din — Saladin — abolished the Fatimid caliphate and restored Egypt to Sunni allegiance under the Abbasids' nominal authority. Yet the Fatimids' legacy endures: Cairo, the city they founded, grew into the largest metropolis of the medieval Arab world, and al-Azhar remains a global center of Islamic scholarship. Their two and a half centuries of rule left an indelible mark on Egypt's identity.

## Key takeaways

- The Fatimids (909–1171) were an Ismaili Shi'a dynasty that challenged Abbasid rule from North Africa.
- They conquered Egypt in 969 and founded Cairo, "the Victorious," as their imperial capital.
- Al-Azhar, founded in 970, became one of Islam's greatest and longest-lived centers of learning.
- Saladin ended the dynasty in 1171, but Cairo remains their enduring monument.
