# The Mongols, the Mamluks, and Ain Jalut

In the mid-13th century, the Mongol Empire — the largest land empire in history — seemed unstoppable. Its armies had crushed Persia, Russia, and Eastern Europe, and in 1258 they destroyed Baghdad, the seat of the Abbasid caliphate. Yet two years later, at a spring in Palestine called Ain Jalut, the seemingly invincible Mongol advance was halted for the first time. The victors were the Mamluks, the remarkable slave-soldiers who ruled Egypt and Syria.

## The Fall of Baghdad (1258)

In 1258, Hulagu Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, led a vast Mongol army against Baghdad, then the greatest city of the Islamic world and capital of the Abbasids for five centuries. After a short siege, the city fell. The Mongols sacked Baghdad with terrible thoroughness: libraries were destroyed, scholars killed, and the Caliph al-Musta'sim was executed. The fall of the caliphate — the symbolic center of Sunni Islam — sent shockwaves through the Muslim world. Syria fell next, and by 1260 Mongol forces stood poised to take Egypt, the last great Muslim power in the region.

## The Rise of the Mamluks

Egypt was ruled by the Mamluks, an elite military caste of Turkish and Circassian slave-soldiers who had seized power in 1250. Trained from boyhood in horsemanship, archery, and warfare, the Mamluks were among the finest cavalry in the world — and, crucially, they had no intention of submitting to the Mongols. When Hulagu demanded Egypt's surrender, the Mamluk sultan Qutuz had the Mongol envoys executed and marched his army north to meet the invaders, joined by his brilliant general Baybars.

## The Battle of Ain Jalut (1260)

On September 3, 1260, the two armies met at Ain Jalut — the "Spring of Goliath" — in what is now northern Israel. The Mongol force, weakened because Hulagu had withdrawn most of his troops to deal with a succession crisis back east, was commanded by his general Kitbuqa. In a hard-fought battle, the Mamluks used a feigned retreat to draw the Mongols into an ambush, and Qutuz himself fought in the front lines. Kitbuqa was killed, and the Mongol army was destroyed. It was the first major defeat the Mongols had ever suffered in a pitched battle — and it stopped their westward expansion permanently.

## Why It Mattered

Ain Jalut saved the Islamic heartlands. Had Egypt fallen, the entire Muslim world from North Africa to India might have shared Baghdad's fate. Instead, the Mamluks went on to expel the remaining Crusaders from the Syrian coast and ruled Egypt and Syria for over two and a half centuries, presiding over a brilliant age of architecture, scholarship, and trade centered on Cairo. Baybars, who succeeded Qutuz shortly after the battle, became one of Islam's great heroes — the sultan who turned back the Mongol tide.

## Key takeaways

- The Mongols destroyed Baghdad in 1258, ending five centuries of Abbasid rule and shocking the Muslim world.
- The Mamluks were elite slave-soldiers who had seized power in Egypt in 1250.
- At Ain Jalut on September 3, 1260, the Mamluks inflicted the Mongols' first great battlefield defeat.
- The victory preserved Egypt and Syria, allowing Mamluk Cairo to become the new center of the Islamic world.
