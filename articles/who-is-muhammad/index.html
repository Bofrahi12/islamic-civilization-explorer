01

The man before the message

Before we speak of revelation, we want you to meet the man. Muhammad was born in Mecca, in western Arabia, around 570 CE — we say 'around' because the exact year comes from later historical reconstruction, not from the Quran. Orphaned young and raised by his uncle, he earned a reputation so complete that his townspeople called him al-Amin, 'the trustworthy.' Even those who later opposed his message never accused him of dishonesty.

A closer distinction

This matters to us for a simple reason: when he began speaking of revelation at forty, his community was not listening to a stranger. They were listening to a man whose character they had tested for decades. Our sources — the Quran, hadith reports, and early biographies, each carrying a different weight — all agree on this starting point. Keep that source distinction in mind as we go: the Quran is revelation for us; the rest is human testimony about a human life.

02

The first revelation

We believe that in the month of Ramadan, while withdrawing to the cave of Hira outside Mecca for reflection, Muhammad ﷺ received the first words of the Quran through the Angel Gabriel: 'Read, in the name of your Lord who created' (96:1–5). He returned home trembling, and his wife Khadijah — the first believer — comforted him and affirmed what she knew of his character. That response, from the person who knew him best, has always moved us.

A closer distinction

The account of the first revelation comes to us through hadith reports, including the well-known narration in Sahih al-Bukhari describing Gabriel's embrace and the Prophet's ﷺ awe. We present this as our tradition's memory, graded and transmitted by our scholars — not as a verse of the Quran. Revelation then continued for about twenty-three years, shaping a community verse by verse, situation by situation.

03

Thirteen years in Mecca: patience under pressure

For thirteen years in Mecca, the early Muslims were a small, vulnerable minority. They faced ridicule, boycott, and violence — and our Prophet ﷺ answered, in those years, with patience and persistence rather than force. The Quranic chapters from this period return again and again to the same themes: God's oneness, the reality of judgment, and comfort for the oppressed. Surah Ad-Duha (93), revealed when revelation briefly paused, speaks to the Prophet ﷺ himself with extraordinary tenderness: 'Your Lord has not forsaken you, nor does He hate you' (93:3).

A closer distinction

Historians — Muslim and non-Muslim — describe these Meccan years as the moral foundation of the movement: a community formed not by power but by conviction. We do not ask you to take our word for any of this uncritically. The early biographies (sira) were compiled generations later and must be read with care; modern historians debate details. What is well established is the shape of the story: a persecuted minority, a message of one God, and a leader who refused to abandon it.

04

Medina: a community takes shape

In 622 CE, Muhammad ﷺ and his followers migrated to Medina — the hijra, the event from which our calendar counts. There he did something remarkable: he built a plural community. The Charter of Medina, a document historians widely regard as authentic in substance, bound Muslims, Jews, and other tribes into one political community with mutual obligations. Whatever one thinks of later history, this founding act was an attempt at coexistence under law.

A closer distinction

The Medinan years brought treaties, conflicts, and eventually the peaceful return to Mecca. We will not pretend every episode is simple — the sources themselves record difficult decisions, and honest history does not flatten them. Our approach is the same one we ask of you: distinguish the Quran's principles from the contested details of seventh-century politics, and judge the whole picture rather than a single decontextualized report.

You may also read Stories of the Prophets for a connected perspective.

05

His character: why we love him

The Quran describes him as being 'of exalted character' (68:4) and as 'a mercy to the worlds' (21:107). Our hadith literature preserves thousands of glimpses: his gentleness with children, his forgiveness of enemies — including the people of Mecca at the moment of his triumph over them — his simplicity of life. His wife Aisha is reported to have said, when asked about his character, 'His character was the Quran' — a report found in collections including Musnad Ahmad and regarded as authentic by many of our scholars.

A closer distinction

We mention these reports with their sources because that is how our tradition works: every admiring story is attached to a chain of transmission that scholars graded. Some reports are strong, some are weak, and we do not build doctrine on weak ones. What emerges from the strongest material is a consistent portrait — truthful before revelation, merciful after it — and that consistency is, for us, part of the evidence.

06

The final prophet — a belief, stated plainly

The Quran calls Muhammad 'the Messenger of God and the Seal of the Prophets' (33:40). For us, this means prophethood ends with him: no new scripture, no new law-bearing messenger after him. This is a statement of our faith, and we make no apology for stating it as belief rather than as something history can prove. Beliefs are not laboratory results; they are commitments about the meaning of a life and a message.

A closer distinction

If you are exploring our faith, we suggest you meet him the way we did: through the Quran first, then through the most reliable reports of his life, always asking which source is speaking. You do not have to accept our conclusions to recognize the seriousness of the claim — that an unlettered man in seventh-century Arabia produced a book that transformed a civilization, and attributed none of it to himself. That, at least, is worth your honest investigation.
