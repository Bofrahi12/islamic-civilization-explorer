# The Crusades from the Muslim Perspective

In the West, the Crusades are often told as a story of knights and holy war. In the Muslim world, they were experienced very differently: as a long series of invasions by foreign armies — the *Franj*, or Franks — that seized Jerusalem and the Syrian coast and held them for nearly two centuries. The Muslim response, from fragmented resistance to united counterattack under Saladin, became one of the defining chapters of medieval Islamic history.

## The First Crusade and the Fall of Jerusalem (1099)

When the First Crusade arrived in the Levant in 1097, the Muslim world was politically divided between the Sunni Seljuk Turks and the Shi'a Fatimid caliphate of Egypt, with local princes ruling the Syrian cities. This disunity proved fatal. After a brutal siege, the Crusaders stormed Jerusalem in July 1099 and massacred much of its Muslim and Jewish population — an atrocity that Muslim chroniclers recorded with horror. The invaders then established the Crusader states: the Kingdom of Jerusalem and the counties of Edessa, Tripoli, and Antioch.

## Saladin and the Road to Hattin

For decades, Muslim resistance was piecemeal. The turning point came with the Zengid rulers of Aleppo and Damascus, especially Nur al-Din, who framed the struggle as a religious duty. His general's nephew, Salah al-Din — known in the West as Saladin — rose to power in Egypt, ended Fatimid rule, and united Egypt and Syria under his command. Saladin spent years preparing: building alliances, training armies, and presenting himself as the champion of Islam against the occupiers.

## The Recapture of Jerusalem (1187)

On July 4, 1187, Saladin's army trapped the Crusader forces at the Horns of Hattin near Tiberias, destroying the Kingdom of Jerusalem's field army in a single day. With the Crusader military broken, city after city surrendered. On October 2, 1187, Jerusalem itself capitulated. In deliberate contrast to the bloodbath of 1099, Saladin spared the city's Christian population, allowing those who could pay a ransom to leave freely and freeing many who could not. His chivalry toward his enemies — including his respectful treatment of the captive King Guy and his dealings with Richard the Lionheart during the Third Crusade — became legendary in both Muslim and Christian traditions.

## Muslim Chroniclers and Memory

The Crusades were recorded by outstanding Muslim historians and writers. Ibn al-Athir's universal chronicle gives the fullest Muslim narrative of the wars; Imad al-Din al-Isfahani, Saladin's own secretary, wrote a vivid eyewitness account of the reconquest; and the Syrian prince Usama ibn Munqidh left memoirs full of sharp, often wry observations about Frankish customs. In Arab memory, Saladin remains the supreme hero of resistance — a Kurdish outsider who united the Muslim world and restored Jerusalem.

## Key takeaways

- The First Crusade captured Jerusalem in 1099 after exploiting divisions among Muslim rulers.
- Saladin united Egypt and Syria and destroyed the Crusader army at Hattin on July 4, 1187.
- Jerusalem was retaken on October 2, 1187, with Saladin notably sparing the city's Christians.
- Muslim chroniclers like Ibn al-Athir and Usama ibn Munqidh provide a rich record of the era from the other side.
