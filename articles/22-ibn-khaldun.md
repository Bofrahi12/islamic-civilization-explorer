# Ibn Khaldun: The Father of Sociology

Long before sociology existed as an academic discipline, a North African scholar moving between the courts and deserts of the medieval Maghreb laid its foundations. Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) analyzed how societies rise and fall with a rigor and originality that still astonish readers more than six centuries later.

## From Tunis to the Courts of North Africa

Born in Tunis in 1332 to a family of Andalusian Arab origin, Ibn Khaldun received a thorough classical education in Quranic studies, jurisprudence, logic, and philosophy. His adult life was a restless journey through the political turbulence of his age: he served the Marinid sultans of Fez, the Hafsid rulers of Tunis, and the Nasrid court of Granada in Muslim Spain, where he formed a close friendship with the statesman and historian Ibn al-Khatib. In his later years he settled in Cairo, where the Mamluk sultan Barquq appointed him chief judge of the Maliki school of law. In 1401, during Timur's siege of Damascus, Ibn Khaldun met the conqueror in person and left a vivid eyewitness account of the encounter.

## The Muqaddima: A New Science of Society

Ibn Khaldun's masterpiece, the Muqaddima ("Introduction"), was composed in 1377 during a period of retreat in a fortified village in present-day Algeria. It was conceived as the opening volume of his universal history, the Kitab al-Ibar ("Book of Lessons"). In it he proposed an entirely new field of inquiry — ilm al-umran, the science of human civilization — devoted to discovering the underlying laws that govern how societies form, prosper, and decay. He insisted that historians must look beyond mere chronicles of kings and battles and examine economic conditions, geography, and social organization.

## Asabiyyah: The Engine of History

At the heart of his theory lies asabiyyah, usually translated as group solidarity or social cohesion. Ibn Khaldun argued that a powerful asabiyyah — the fierce mutual loyalty found among desert and tribal peoples — enables a group to conquer settled lands and found a dynasty. Yet success carries the seeds of decline: wealth, luxury, and urban comfort gradually erode that solidarity over the course of three or four generations, until a new vigorous group rises to replace the old. History, in his view, turns in great cycles of vigor and decadence. Along the way he offered striking observations on taxation, the division of labor, and the idea that labor is the true source of economic value.

## Legacy

Rediscovered by European scholars in the 19th century, Ibn Khaldun is now recognized as a thinker centuries ahead of his time, anticipating modern sociology, economics, demography, and the philosophy of history. His work remains one of the most original intellectual achievements of the medieval world, Muslim or otherwise.

## Key takeaways

- Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), born in Tunis, effectively founded the science of society centuries before modern sociology.
- His Muqaddima (1377) argued that history follows discoverable laws rooted in social and economic conditions.
- The concept of asabiyyah (group solidarity) powers his famous cyclical theory of dynastic rise and fall.
- A courtier, judge, and diplomat, he served rulers across North Africa and Spain and met Timur in 1401.
