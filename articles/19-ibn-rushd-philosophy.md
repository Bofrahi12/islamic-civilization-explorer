# Ibn Rushd (Averroes): Philosophy and Reason

In medieval Paris, scholars debating the deepest questions of existence referred to Aristotle simply as "the Philosopher" — and to his greatest interpreter as "the Commentator." That commentator was Ibn Rushd of Cordoba, known in the West as Averroes, whose defense of reason against dogma made him one of the most controversial and influential thinkers of the Middle Ages.

## The Commentator of Cordoba

Born in Cordoba in 1126 into a distinguished family of jurists, Ibn Rushd mastered Islamic law, medicine, astronomy, and philosophy, eventually serving as court physician and chief judge under the Almohad caliphs. His monumental project was a series of commentaries on nearly all of Aristotle's works — short, middle, and long commentaries that explained, systematized, and defended Aristotelian thought with unmatched rigor. For centuries, the Latin West would learn Aristotle largely through Averroes.

## Faith and Reason

Ibn Rushd's boldest work addressed the tension between philosophy and religion. In *The Decisive Treatise*, he argued that philosophy and revelation cannot truly conflict, since both lead to truth; where scripture appears to contradict demonstrated reason, it should be interpreted allegorically. He insisted that the study of philosophy was not merely permitted but religiously obligatory for those capable of it. These views provoked fierce opposition from theologians such as al-Ghazali, whom Ibn Rushd answered in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*.

## The Latin Averroists

Translated into Latin and Hebrew, Ibn Rushd's works electrified 13th-century Europe. At the University of Paris, a movement known as Latin Averroism — associated with thinkers like Siger of Brabant — championed his ideas about reason and the eternity of the world, provoking condemnations from church authorities and responses from Thomas Aquinas, who engaged with Averroes seriously even while refuting him. Through this controversy, Ibn Rushd became a central figure in the formation of Western scholastic philosophy.

## Key takeaways

- Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle were so authoritative that medieval Europe called him simply "the Commentator."
- He argued that philosophy and faith are compatible, and that reason must guide the interpretation of scripture.
- His *Incoherence of the Incoherence* defended philosophy against al-Ghazali's attack.
- Latin translations of his work sparked the Averroist movement in Paris and profoundly shaped Western medieval philosophy.
