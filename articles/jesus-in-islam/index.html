01

Jesus in our faith: prophet and Messiah

For us, Jesus — Isa in Arabic — is one of the greatest prophets and messengers of God, and the Messiah. Our Quran introduces him as 'the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary,' honored in this world and the next (3:45). That combination may surprise you: we affirm the title Messiah while understanding it differently from Christian theology. For us it means the anointed one sent to the Children of Israel with guidance and signs — a human servant of God, not God Himself.

A closer distinction

This is not a marginal belief for us. Jesus is mentioned by name many times in our Quran, and his story is told with evident love. We want you to hear that first, before any discussion of disagreement: whatever divides our theologies, it does not divide our reverence. A Muslim who speaks lightly of Jesus has misunderstood his own faith.

02

Mary: a woman honored above all

Our Quran devotes an entire chapter to Maryam — Mary — and tells her story with tenderness and detail (19:16–36). An angel announces to her a pure son, and she asks how this can be, having known no man. The answer comes: it is easy for God, who says 'Be,' and it is. When she withdraws to give birth alone, God comforts her, provides for her, and the newborn Jesus speaks from the cradle to defend her honor.

A closer distinction

The Quran declares that God chose Mary 'above the women of the worlds' (3:42). For us, she is the model of devotion, courage, and trust — a young woman who faced her community's suspicion with dignity because she trusted God's word. If you come from a Christian background, you may find our portrait of Mary strikingly familiar in its reverence, even as our theology of her son differs.

03

His miracles — always 'by God's permission'

We believe Jesus performed astonishing miracles: healing the blind and the leper, raising the dead, even fashioning a bird of clay and breathing life into it (3:49; 5:110). But every account in our Quran carries the same refrain — 'by My permission.' That phrase is the key to our whole understanding. The miracles are real signs, but the power belongs to God alone, granted through His messenger as proof of his truthfulness.

A closer distinction

This is how we read all prophetic miracles: Moses' staff, Jesus' healings, our Prophet's ﷺ revelation — none of them make the prophet divine. They are God's acts, done through human hands. So when we honor Jesus' miracles, we are honoring both the messenger and the One who sent him. The distinction matters to us because worship, in our faith, belongs to God alone.

04

Where we differ: the cross

We know this is the sensitive point, and we do not raise it lightly. The Quran addresses the crucifixion directly: those who claimed to have killed Jesus 'did not kill him, nor did they crucify him — but it was made to appear so to them' (4:157). 'Rather, God raised him to Himself' (4:158). We read this as a clear denial that Jesus died on the cross, and an affirmation that God rescued and raised him.

A closer distinction

We are careful about what the verse does and does not specify. It does not give a detailed alternative narrative — it does not name a substitute or describe the mechanics. Classical scholars proposed various explanations, and we distinguish the Quran's certain statement from later scholarly speculation, which we do not present as doctrine. We discuss this with Christians 'in the best manner' (29:46), because disagreement need not mean disrespect — and we ask only to be heard accurately in return.

05

We do not worship Jesus — and why that honors him

Our faith teaches the worship of the One God alone, and this is where our love of Jesus takes a form Christians may find unfamiliar. We believe that calling Jesus God would actually lower him — because in our understanding, the highest possible rank for a human being is to be God's chosen servant and messenger. Prophets are honored precisely because they are human beings who submitted completely to God.

A closer distinction

The Quran records Jesus himself, on the Day of Judgment, disavowing any claim to divinity: he will say he told people only to worship God, his Lord and their Lord (5:116–118). Whether or not you accept our scripture, we hope you can see the internal coherence: our refusal to worship Jesus comes from the same devotion that makes us love him. God's oneness — tawhid — is the thread that runs through everything we believe about him.

06

His return: a story still unfolding

Our disagreement about the cross does not diminish Jesus' role in our faith — in some ways it enlarges it. We believe Jesus was raised alive to God and will return before the end of time. The Prophet ﷺ said, in reports recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari (3448) and Sahih Muslim (155) — two of our most trusted hadith collections — that the son of Mary will descend as a just judge. The Quran hints at this too: Jesus is 'a sign of the Hour' (43:61).

A closer distinction

In our tradition, his return will confirm the oneness of God and the finality of Muhammad's ﷺ message. For us, Jesus is not a finished chapter; his story continues, and we await its completion with hope. If you take one thing from this guide, let it be this: we do not deny Jesus — we deny the crucifixion while affirming his messiahship, his miracles, his virgin birth, and his return. That is the shape of our love for him.
