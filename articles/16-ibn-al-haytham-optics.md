# Ibn al-Haytham and the Science of Light

Long before the modern laboratory existed, a scholar in Cairo was testing theories about light with darkened rooms and careful observation. Ibn al-Haytham — known in the Latin West as Alhazen — wrote the *Book of Optics*, a work that demolished a thousand-year-old theory of vision and insisted that claims about nature must be proven by experiment.

## From Basra to Cairo

Born in Basra (in present-day Iraq) in 965, Ibn al-Haytham was educated in the sciences of his day and gained a reputation for brilliance. He was summoned to Cairo by the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim, who had heard of his idea for regulating the floods of the Nile. When Ibn al-Haytham realized the engineering project was beyond the technology of his time, he reportedly feigned madness to avoid the caliph's wrath, living under house arrest until the caliph's death. It was during this seclusion that he produced his greatest works.

## The Book of Optics

In the *Kitab al-Manazir* (Book of Optics), Ibn al-Haytham took on the dominant ancient theories of vision. Greek thinkers such as Euclid and Ptolemy had argued that the eye sends out rays to "touch" objects — the so-called extramission theory. Ibn al-Haytham argued the opposite: light travels from objects into the eye in straight lines. He backed this with experiments, including early versions of the camera obscura — a darkened chamber (*qamara*) with a small hole through which an inverted image of the outside world is projected. His work covered reflection, refraction, lenses, and the anatomy of the eye.

## A Pioneer of Experiment

What set Ibn al-Haytham apart was his method. He insisted that mathematical reasoning about nature had to be checked against controlled observation, and he designed experiments that isolated one variable at a time. Historians of science often describe him as an early pioneer of the experimental method that would later define modern science. His *Book of Optics* was translated into Latin in the 13th century and studied by European scholars including Roger Bacon and Johannes Kepler, shaping the development of optics in the West for centuries.

## Key takeaways

- Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics* proved that vision works by light entering the eye, overturning the ancient extramission theory.
- He used the camera obscura and controlled experiments to test his ideas about light, reflection, and refraction.
- He is widely regarded as an early pioneer of the experimental scientific method.
- His work, translated into Latin, directly influenced European optics from the Middle Ages to Kepler.
