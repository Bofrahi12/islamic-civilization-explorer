The Character of Muhammad ﷺ: Mercy, Honesty, and Strength

8 min read · 2026-09-27 · Category: Prophets

Beyond biography: the character Muslims love — his mercy to enemies, his honesty, his tenderness with the vulnerable — told through Quran and authentic hadith.

Why character is the argument

When Muslims are asked why they love Muhammad ﷺ, we rarely begin with battles or dates. We begin with character. Our Quran describes him in two phrases we return to constantly: he is 'a mercy to the worlds' (21:107) and he stands upon 'tremendous character' (68:4). For us, his life is the lived commentary on the Quran's teachings.

Mercy even toward his enemies

The moment that defines him for us came at Taif, where a hostile town stoned him until he bled. The angel of the mountains offered to crush the town in revenge — and he refused, saying instead that he hoped their descendants would worship God (Bukhari 3231). Years later, entering Makkah as a conqueror over the people who had persecuted, boycotted, and tried to kill him, he declared a general amnesty: 'Go, for you are free.'

Honesty before prophethood

Long before revelation, Makkah knew him as Al-Amin — the Trustworthy. Merchants left their wealth with him; disputing tribes chose him to arbitrate. When he first gathered his people to warn them, he asked: 'If I told you an army was behind this hill, would you believe me?' They answered: 'We have never known you to lie.' Only then did he deliver his message.

Tenderness with the vulnerable

His gentleness showed most with those the world overlooks. He shortened his prayer when he heard a child crying, not wanting to burden the mother. He played with his grandchildren, mended his own clothes, and sat with the poor as equals. 'None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself' (Bukhari 13) — we hear in this the whole of his social ethic.

Meet him through his life and words

No article can substitute for meeting him through the sources: the Quran's portrait, the authentic hadith collections, and the early biographies. Read them the way we do — looking not for a flawless superhero but for a human being whose every recorded instinct bent toward mercy, truth, and God.
