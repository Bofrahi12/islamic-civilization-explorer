# The Mughal Empire

Founded in 1526 by the Central Asian prince Babur, the Mughal Empire grew into one of the largest and most culturally vibrant states in world history. At its height around 1700, it governed most of the Indian subcontinent and was among the richest empires on Earth. Its legacy — in architecture, language, cuisine, and administration — still shapes South Asia today.

## Babur's Conquest

Babur, a descendant of Timur on his father's side and of Genghis Khan on his mother's, was a Timurid prince from the Fergana Valley in Central Asia. After losing his ancestral lands, he turned south and won a decisive victory at the Battle of Panipat in 1526, where his smaller force defeated the much larger army of the Delhi Sultanate with the help of field artillery and cavalry tactics. Within a year he had taken Delhi and Agra, laying the foundations of Mughal rule. Babur died in 1530, but his memoir, the *Baburnama*, survives as one of the great autobiographies of world literature.

## Akbar the Great: Administration and Tolerance

Babur's grandson Akbar (reigned 1556–1605) is widely regarded as the true architect of the empire. He built a centralized administration based on a ranking system for officials, reformed taxation, and kept detailed land records. Unlike many rulers of his age, he pursued a policy of religious tolerance: he married Rajput princesses, appointed Hindus to high office, abolished the *jizya* tax on non-Muslims, and hosted debates between Muslim, Hindu, Christian, and Jain scholars at his court. His court historian Abul Fazl recorded these policies in the *Ain-i-Akbari*, a remarkable administrative encyclopedia.

## Shah Jahan and the Taj Mahal

Under Shah Jahan (reigned 1628–1658), Mughal art and architecture reached their peak. The emperor commissioned the Taj Mahal in Agra as a mausoleum for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631. Built over roughly two decades by thousands of artisans and craftsmen from across Asia, the white-marble monument with its great dome and inlaid decoration became the most famous building in India and a symbol of the empire's refinement. Shah Jahan also rebuilt Delhi, creating the Red Fort and the great congregational mosque of Shahjahanabad.

## Decline and Lasting Legacy

After the long reign of Aurangzeb (1658–1707), the empire gradually weakened under pressure from regional powers, European trading companies, and internal rebellion. British power ended the dynasty formally in 1857. Yet the Mughal legacy endures: the Urdu language, much of North Indian cuisine, garden and architectural design across the subcontinent, and administrative practices adopted by later states all trace back to this remarkable imperial age.

## Key takeaways

- The Mughal Empire was founded in 1526 at Panipat and lasted, in name, until 1857.
- Akbar's tolerant administration and merit-based bureaucracy were the empire's political foundation.
- The Taj Mahal, built under Shah Jahan, remains the most iconic monument of Islamic architecture in South Asia.
- Mughal culture fused Persian, Central Asian, and Indian traditions, leaving a permanent mark on the region.
