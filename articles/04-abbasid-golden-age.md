# The Abbasid Golden Age

In 750, the Abbasids overthrew the Umayyads and inaugurated what is often called the Golden Age of Islam. Shifting the center of power eastward to Iraq, they presided over two centuries of extraordinary cultural and scientific achievement. At its heart stood Baghdad, a new imperial capital that became the largest and most intellectually vibrant city in the world.

## The Founding of Baghdad

The second Abbasid caliph, al-Mansur, founded his capital around 762 on the Tigris River, naming it Madinat al-Salam, the City of Peace. Baghdad was laid out as a perfect circle, with the caliph's palace and the great mosque at its center and markets and neighborhoods radiating outward — a physical expression of an ordered, divinely sanctioned empire. Its location was inspired: the Tigris linked it to the Persian Gulf and Indian Ocean trade, while canals and roads tied it to Syria, Arabia, and Central Asia. Within a generation, it drew merchants, scholars, and artisans from across the known world, growing into a metropolis of several hundred thousand people.

## Harun al-Rashid and the Court

The caliph Harun al-Rashid, who reigned from 786 to 809, became the emblem of Abbasid splendor — the caliph immortalized in the tales of the Thousand and One Nights. His court glittered with poets, musicians, and scholars, and his wealth became legendary across three continents; he famously exchanged embassies and gifts with Charlemagne. Beneath the pageantry, his reign was one of competent administration and prosperity: secure trade routes, full treasuries, and ambitious public works. It was under Harun and his son al-Ma'mun that Baghdad's intellectual life reached its zenith.

## The House of Wisdom and the Translation Movement

The Abbasids' most enduring achievement was the great translation movement centered on the Bayt al-Hikma, the House of Wisdom, in Baghdad. Caliphs paid scholars — Muslim, Christian, and Jewish alike — to translate the accumulated knowledge of antiquity into Arabic: the philosophy of Plato and Aristotle, the medicine of Galen and Hippocrates, the mathematics and astronomy of India and Persia. Translators were rewarded with the weight of their books in gold, according to tradition. The adoption of paper, learned from Chinese prisoners after the Battle of Talas in 751, made books cheap for the first time in history, and Baghdad's booksellers' quarter bustled with hundreds of shops.

## Science and Scholarship

What began as translation became original discovery. Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, working in Baghdad, wrote the foundational treatise on algebra — the word itself comes from "al-jabr" in his book's title, and "algorithm" derives from his name. Physicians systematized Greek medicine and advanced surgery and pharmacology; astronomers built observatories and refined the instruments of navigation; geographers mapped the known world. Much of this knowledge would later flow into Europe through Spain and Sicily, helping to seed the Renaissance. Political fragmentation eventually dimmed Baghdad's star, but the intellectual tradition the Abbasids fostered shaped science for centuries.

## Key takeaways

- The Abbasids founded Baghdad around 762, which became the world's largest and most learned city.
- Harun al-Rashid's reign (786–809) symbolizes the peak of Abbasid wealth, culture, and court life.
- The House of Wisdom's translation movement preserved ancient knowledge and made it available in Arabic.
- Abbasid scholars pioneered algebra, advanced medicine and astronomy, and later transmitted this learning to Europe.
