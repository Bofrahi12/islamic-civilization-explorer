# The Ottoman Empire

The Ottoman Empire endured for more than six hundred years — one of the longest-lived empires in history. Beginning as a tiny frontier principality in Anatolia around 1299, it grew to rule southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa, bridging the medieval and modern worlds and shaping the map and cultures of three continents.

## Origins in Anatolia

The empire takes its name from Osman, a Turkish chieftain who led a small band of ghazi warriors on the Byzantine frontier in northwestern Anatolia. His successors were ambitious and capable: they captured the Byzantine city of Bursa, crossed into Europe, and built a disciplined army centered on infantry and, increasingly, gunpowder weapons. By the late fourteenth century, the Ottomans controlled much of the Balkans, reducing Byzantium to little more than Constantinople itself. A crushing defeat by the Central Asian conqueror Timur in 1402 nearly destroyed the young state, but the Ottomans recovered with remarkable speed.

## Mehmed II and the Fall of Constantinople

In 1453, the twenty-one-year-old Sultan Mehmed II achieved what no Muslim ruler had managed in eight centuries: he captured Constantinople. After a fifty-three-day siege featuring massive cannon — among the largest ever built — the ancient capital of Byzantium fell. Mehmed, thereafter called "the Conqueror," made the city his capital, renaming it Istanbul, and set about rebuilding it as an imperial metropolis: repopulating it, endowing mosques and markets, and inviting scholars and artisans. The fall of Constantinople is conventionally taken as the end of the Middle Ages, and it announced the Ottomans as a world power.

## Suleiman the Magnificent

Under Suleiman, who reigned from 1520 to 1566, the empire reached its greatest extent and cultural height. His armies took Belgrade, Rhodes, and Hungary, and his fleets dominated the Mediterranean; at its peak the empire touched three continents and ruled over tens of millions of people. Known in Turkish as Kanuni, "the Lawgiver," Suleiman systematized Ottoman law and administration. His reign was also a golden age of art and architecture: the great architect Sinan built the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul, a masterpiece of domed space that rivals the Byzantine Hagia Sophia. Poetry, calligraphy, and ceramics flourished under imperial patronage.

## Administration and Legacy

The Ottomans governed their vast, diverse empire with a sophisticated bureaucracy. The sultan ruled as absolute monarch, advised by viziers and supported by an elite administration; non-Muslim communities were organized into millets, self-governing religious communities that managed their own laws and worship. The devshirme system recruited talented Christian youths into imperial service, producing some of the empire's greatest generals and grand viziers. After Suleiman, the empire gradually lost its military edge to European rivals, though it remained a great power for centuries. Defeat in World War I brought its end: the sultanate was abolished in 1922, and the Turkish Republic rose from its Anatolian heartland. The Ottoman legacy — in law, architecture, cuisine, and the borders of the modern Middle East — remains visible today.

## Key takeaways

- Founded around 1299 by Osman, the Ottoman Empire lasted over six centuries, one of history's longest-lived states.
- Mehmed II's conquest of Constantinople in 1453 made Istanbul the imperial capital and ended the Byzantine Empire.
- Suleiman the Magnificent (1520–1566) brought the empire to its greatest extent and a golden age of law and architecture.
- Ottoman institutions, from the millet system to Sinan's mosques, shaped the Middle East, the Balkans, and beyond.
