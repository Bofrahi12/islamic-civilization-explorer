# The Rashidun Caliphs

After the Prophet Muhammad's death in 632, the Muslim community faced an urgent question: who would lead? The four men chosen in succession — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali — are known as the Rashidun, or "Rightly Guided" Caliphs. In just three decades, they preserved the young faith, carried it beyond Arabia, and built the administrative foundations of an empire.

## Abu Bakr: Holding the Community Together

Abu Bakr, Muhammad's closest friend and father-in-law, was chosen as the first caliph. His two-year reign was dominated by crisis: with the Prophet gone, several Arabian tribes renounced Islam or followed rival prophets. Abu Bakr responded with the Ridda ("apostasy") wars, campaigns that by 633 had reunited Arabia under Medina's authority. He also authorized the first gathering of the Quran's scattered verses into a single written collection, fearing the words might be lost as those who had memorized them died in battle. When he died in 634, he nominated Umar as his successor, establishing a precedent of designated succession.

## Umar: The Great Expansion

Umar's ten-year caliphate saw one of the most astonishing expansions in history. Muslim armies defeated the Byzantine Empire at Yarmouk in 636, taking Syria and Palestine, and shattered Sassanid Persia at Qadisiyyah, absorbing Iraq and eventually all of Persia. Jerusalem surrendered peacefully, and Egypt fell soon after. Umar matched conquest with administration: he created the diwan, a register for paying soldiers and officials from state revenues; founded garrison cities like Kufa and Basra; and introduced the Islamic Hijri calendar, dating from the Prophet's migration. He was assassinated in 644 by a disgruntled Persian captive, but the state he built proved durable.

## Uthman: The Written Quran

Uthman, an early convert from the wealthy Umayyad clan, is remembered above all for standardizing the Quran. As the empire grew, regional differences in recitation emerged, and Uthman commissioned an authoritative written text, sending copies to the major cities — the basis of the Quran read by Muslims today. His reign continued the expansion into North Africa and Central Asia, but his favoritism toward relatives in appointments bred resentment. In 656, rebels besieged his house in Medina and murdered him, an event that fractured the community and triggered the first civil war in Islamic history.

## Ali and the First Fitna

Ali, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, became caliph amid turmoil. His legitimacy was challenged by those demanding punishment of Uthman's killers, leading to the Battle of the Camel and then the standoff at Siffin against Muawiya, the governor of Syria. An arbitration meant to resolve the dispute only deepened divisions, and a radical faction — the Kharijites — broke away, declaring both sides illegitimate. In 661, a Kharijite assassinated Ali. Muawiya then claimed the caliphate, ending the Rashidun era and founding the Umayyad dynasty. The divisions of this civil war, the first fitna, echo through Islamic history to this day.

## Key takeaways

- The Rashidun caliphs (632–661) preserved Islam after the Prophet's death and expanded it into a Middle Eastern empire.
- Umar's reign created enduring institutions: the diwan, garrison cities, and the Hijri calendar.
- Uthman's standardized text of the Quran remains the authoritative version used by Muslims worldwide.
- The civil war under Ali produced lasting divisions, including the roots of the Sunni-Shi'a split.
