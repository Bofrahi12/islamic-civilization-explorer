01

Do Muslims worship a different God?

No. 'Allah' is simply the Arabic word for God — Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews use it too. When we say Allah, we mean the one Creator and Sustainer of all that exists, without partners or equals. Our Quran's shortest chapter states it with complete directness: 'Say: He is Allah, the One; Allah, the Eternal Refuge; He neither begets nor is born; and there is none like unto Him' (112:1–4).

A closer distinction

The real difference between Islam and some other faiths is not the word but the theology: we affirm God's absolute oneness (tawhid) and direct all worship to Him alone, without intermediaries. If you want the fuller picture, our guide on who Allah is explores the names, attributes, and what this belief changes in a life. That single conviction — one God, worthy of worship alone — is the axis around which everything else in Islam turns.

02

What does Islam actually say about women?

This question can point at several different things — the Quran, hadith, legal interpretation, Muslim history, or the laws of a modern state — and fair answers keep those layers distinct. Our Quran presents women and men as originating from a single soul, addresses women directly as moral and spiritual agents, and promises the same reward: 'Whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while being a believer — We will surely cause them to live a good life' (16:97). It also assigns women specified shares of inheritance (4:7).

A closer distinction

At the same time, the Quran contains gender-differentiated rulings whose interpretation our scholars debate, and Muslim women's lived experience varies enormously across history and culture. Anyone who tells you 'Islam says' one simple thing about women is skipping the evidence. Our longer guide on women in Islam walks through the verses, the disagreements, and the history — including the women who transmitted hadith and taught law. The shortest honest answer is that Islam dignifies women in its scripture while Muslim history, like all history, is mixed — and both facts deserve to be faced.

03

Does Islam promote violence? What is jihad?

The Arabic word jihad means striving or struggling — in our tradition it covers the struggle against one's own faults, the effort to live righteously, and, in a specific legal sense, armed defense under strict conditions. Our Quran permits fighting against those who fight you but commands, in the same verse, 'do not transgress; indeed, Allah does not love transgressors' (2:190). Classical law surrounded warfare with restraints: no targeting of non-combatants, no destruction of property or nature.

A closer distinction

Terrorism against civilians is condemned across our mainstream scholarship, and the overwhelming majority of Muslims live this plainly. That said, honesty requires admitting that verses about fighting exist and were revealed in a historical context of persecution and war — understanding them takes the same care any ancient scripture requires. Claims you see online, for or against us, should always be checked against sources, not slogans. If a headline about 'jihad' alarms you, ask which meaning is being used — the word's breadth is exactly what makes it so easy to misuse.

04

Can the Quran be trusted? Was it preserved?

This is one of the most-asked questions, and it has a two-part answer. Our Quran makes the claim itself: 'Indeed, it is We who sent down the Reminder, and indeed, We will be its guardian' (15:9). The traditional Muslim account is that the text was memorized by the first community, written down during our Prophet's ﷺ lifetime, and compiled into a standard codex under the caliph Uthman — and that millions in every generation since have memorized it word for word in Arabic.

A closer distinction

Academic study of early manuscripts is a separate conversation, and a responsible site presents both: the traditional account and what manuscript research discusses, without erasing either. What is not seriously disputed is the outcome — one Arabic text, recited identically from Jakarta to Dakar. Our guide on how the Quran was preserved goes through the evidence step by step. You do not need to take our word for any of this: the text is public, the recitation is audible worldwide, and the invitation to examine it is open.

You may also read Who Is Allah? for a connected perspective.

05

What do Muslims believe about Jesus?

We love and honor Jesus deeply — just not in the way Christian theology describes him. Our Quran affirms his miraculous birth to Mary, who has an entire chapter named after her; it describes his miracles — healing the sick, raising the dead — as performed by God's permission; and it affirms that he will return. 'The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, was only a messenger of Allah' (4:171) states our position: a mighty prophet and messenger, not divine.

A closer distinction

Where we differ from Christians is theology, not respect. To us, calling a prophet 'God' would lower him, because prophets are honored precisely as devoted human servants of the One God. If this surprises you, you are not alone — it is the single most common discovery for Christian visitors to this site, and our guide on Jesus in Islam explores it with full references and care for Christian readers. That distinction — deep love without divinization — is the key that unlocks the entire Muslim view of Jesus.
