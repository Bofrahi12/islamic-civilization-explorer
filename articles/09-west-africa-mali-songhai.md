# Islam in West Africa: Mali and Songhai

Long before European colonization, West Africa was home to powerful Muslim empires whose wealth and scholarship were known across the Islamic world. Islam arrived with trans-Saharan traders and gradually won over kings and commoners alike. The empires of Mali and Songhai, rising and falling between the 13th and 16th centuries, turned cities like Timbuktu and Gao into centers of learning, law, and commerce.

## The Empire of Mali and the Rise of Islam

The Mali Empire was founded in the early 13th century by Sundiata Keita, who united the Mandinka peoples of the upper Niger region. Islam had already been spreading among merchants and royal courts for generations — some earlier Ghanaian kings had converted — and Mali's rulers embraced the faith while ruling over a diverse population of Muslims and followers of traditional religions. The empire controlled the trade in gold and salt that crossed the Sahara, which made it one of the wealthiest states of its time.

## Mansa Musa's Hajj of 1324

Mali's most famous ruler, Mansa Musa, made the pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 with a caravan of staggering size — chroniclers report tens of thousands of attendants and enormous quantities of gold. His lavish spending in Cairo drew the attention of the entire Mediterranean world; a Catalan map of 1375 even depicts the king of Mali seated on a golden throne. The journey announced West Africa's wealth to the wider world and strengthened Mali's ties with North Africa and the Middle East. On his return, Musa brought scholars, architects, and books back to Mali.

## Timbuktu: City of Scholars

Under Mali and later Songhai, Timbuktu became one of the great intellectual centers of the Islamic world. Its mosques — Djinguereber, Sankore, and Sidi Yahya — doubled as universities where scholars studied law, theology, astronomy, mathematics, and medicine. Thousands of manuscripts, copied and annotated by local scholars, survive to this day, preserving evidence of a vibrant written culture. Families of scholars in Timbuktu kept private libraries, and students traveled from across the region to study there.

## The Songhai Empire under Askia Muhammad

As Mali declined in the 15th century, the Songhai Empire rose to take its place, centered on Gao. Its greatest ruler, Askia Muhammad (reigned 1493–1528), was a devout Muslim who made his own pilgrimage to Mecca, reformed the administration, standardized weights and measures, and appointed judges trained in Islamic law throughout the empire. Songhai reached its greatest extent under him, stretching across much of the western Sahel. The empire fell in 1591 when a Moroccan army crossed the Sahara, but the scholarly tradition of Timbuktu survived the conquest.

## Key takeaways

- The Mali and Songhai empires controlled the lucrative trans-Saharan gold trade and were among the richest states of the medieval world.
- Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca introduced West Africa's wealth and power to the wider Islamic world.
- Timbuktu's mosques and manuscript libraries made it a major center of Islamic scholarship.
- Askia Muhammad's Songhai Empire (1493–1528) brought the region to its political and cultural peak.
